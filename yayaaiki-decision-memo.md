# Decision Memo: YayaAiki Nigeria-First Verified-Work Infrastructure

**Decision:** Proceed only with a **90-day, Nigeria-first, buyer-funded pilot**. Do not fund or market YayaAiki yet as a pan-African marketplace, payment network, or proven infrastructure company. The evidence supports a real problem and a plausible wedge, but not product-market fit, durable economics, or a defensible moat.

The best initial product is a narrow workflow for **repeatable, auditable digital work**—for example AI evaluation, data classification, transcription/translation, or QA—where a buyer defines acceptance criteria, YayaAiki routes work to screened Nigerian professionals, independent review measures completion, and a licensed provider handles payout. The initial customer is an enterprise, AI/data vendor, research firm, NGO, agency, or public program that buys recurring batches. Individual worker acquisition should support this motion, not define it.

## Executive conclusion

| Question | Conclusion | Confidence and condition |
|---|---|---|
| Is the problem real? | **Yes.** Buyers face fragmented quality, identity, evidence, and payout risk; workers face weak proof of informal work and unreliable income rails. | High on problem existence; low on YayaAiki-specific urgency and willingness to pay. |
| Can launch be profitable? | **Possibly, but only in a narrow, high-repeat workflow.** Human review, support, fraud, payment, and compliance costs can erase marketplace margins. | Unproven until contribution margin is measured on paid work orders. |
| Who are the strongest competitors? | Upwork and Workana directly; Andela and Toptal for vetted talent; Jobberman/TATC, TERAWORK, and Gebeya regionally; Deel and Workpay for compliance and payments; BPOs and internal QA teams indirectly. | High confidence that the capability stack is contested. |
| What can be a moat? | A permissioned, longitudinal graph of buyer-confirmed work outcomes, reviewer reliability, disputes, payments, and portable credentials, embedded in customer workflows and backed by local compliance integrations. | Plausible, but it only becomes a moat after meaningful verified volume and trusted data contributors. |
| Launch posture | Nigeria, one vertical, one buyer persona, one or two licensed payout partners, web plus WhatsApp-assisted intake, and manual operations initially. | Recommended. Do not expand country-by-country until metrics clear explicit gates. |

## 1. The problem is real, but the strongest pain is operational rather than inspirational

Online gig demand is growing faster in developing markets. The World Bank reports that postings from Sub-Saharan Africa on the largest digital platform grew 130% versus 14% in North America in its study, and that almost 60% of firms in poorer countries reported increased outsourcing to gig workers.[1] This establishes a credible demand direction, not Nigeria-specific revenue, margins, or liquidity. The same evidence counts 545 platforms across 186 countries and finds that regional and local platforms are numerous but do not necessarily capture proportional traffic, which is a warning against assuming that local supply alone creates a business.[2]

The supply-side problem is also real. Nigeria’s Q2 2024 Labour Force Survey reported 93.0% informal employment and 85.6% self-employment.[3] Many capable workers therefore lack standardized, portable evidence of what they delivered, to what specification, with what outcome. Conventional CVs, testimonials, and platform ratings are often weak signals. A work record that links identity, scope, artifact, timestamps, acceptance, reviewer decisions, payment completion, and dispute history could reduce uncertainty.

The buyer-side pain is more valuable than the worker-side narrative. A business does not primarily pay for a profile or a badge. It pays to reduce **rework, failed delivery, fraud, screening time, payment failure, and dispute cost**. YayaAiki’s stated workflow—work order, assignment, evidence, independent review, payout, and portable history—maps to these costs.[4] It is therefore more credible as a **verified-work operations layer** than as a general freelancer community.

The problem is not uniformly accessible across Africa. GSMA reports only 27% mobile-internet penetration in Sub-Saharan Africa at the end of 2023 and a 60% usage gap, with device affordability and digital skills as material constraints.[5] A web and WhatsApp entry point can reduce friction, but it does not solve device sharing, electricity, bandwidth, literacy, language, or identity-document constraints. A smartphone-only or high-bandwidth product would exclude part of the potential supply.

Nigeria has meaningful payment infrastructure. The Central Bank of Nigeria reported 22.42 billion electronic-payment transactions worth NGN1.559 quadrillion in January–June 2024, including 11.64 billion internet-transfer transactions.[6] These figures show that digital rails exist; they do **not** show that freelance payments are large, cheap, reliable for this use case, or available for YayaAiki’s unlicensed custody. EFInA reports that 31% of Nigerian adults received digital income payments and that irregular or insufficient income remained a major barrier.[7] Worker supply does not imply worker ability or willingness to pay fees.

**Decision implication:** Treat the problem as validated at the category level, but treat YayaAiki’s specific buyer pain, pricing, and repeat demand as hypotheses requiring paid pilots.

## 2. Can launch be profitable? A simple unit-economics test

Profitability is possible if YayaAiki sells a sufficiently valuable, repeatable outcome and keeps human verification cost below the value of the transaction. It is unlikely if the company subsidizes low-value tasks, pays for broad worker acquisition, or treats payment volume as revenue.

For each completed work order, use the following contribution-margin equation:

> **Contribution margin per work order = buyer revenue − worker payout − reviewer cost − fraud/rework/dispute reserve − payment and FX cost − support/operations cost − variable infrastructure cost.**

A practical pricing model should begin with one transaction, not a complex network. Suppose, purely as an **illustrative planning assumption**, a buyer pays **NGN100,000** for a batch. If the worker receives 65% (NGN65,000), independent review costs NGN12,000, payment/FX costs NGN3,000, fraud and rework reserve is NGN5,000, and support/operations cost is NGN8,000, contribution is NGN7,000, or 7%. That is too thin for customer acquisition, legal work, product development, and management overhead. If the same workflow can charge NGN140,000 for the same worker payout and reduce review plus support through calibrated automation and repeatable standards, contribution becomes materially healthier. These numbers are not market facts; they are a sensitivity example showing why task selection matters.

The business should therefore target work with four properties: recurring volume, objective acceptance criteria, sufficient buyer value per batch, and a low enough error rate that review cost declines with learning. A task that pays NGN10,000 but requires a human reviewer, multiple disputes, and payment reconciliation may be structurally unattractive even if demand is large. A task that saves a buyer a failed-delivery incident or reduces a multi-day staffing process can support a higher fee.

YayaAiki should test three revenue forms, in sequence. First, charge a per-work-order orchestration and verification fee or a transparent take rate. Second, add a buyer subscription only when a customer needs recurring queues, analytics, access controls, and audit exports. Third, offer managed operations or API access for enterprise customers with repeat demand. Worker-paid profile or verification fees should not be the core model; they risk excluding cash-constrained workers and can create incentives to monetize registration rather than outcomes.

**Profitability gate before fundraising:** at least three paying repeat customers; a positive contribution margin on a cohort of completed work orders after worker payout, review, support, fraud, and payment costs; and evidence that margin does not depend on founder-led exception handling. Track median revenue per order, payout percentage, review minutes per order, rework percentage, dispute loss, payment failure, support minutes, customer acquisition cost, and repeat-order gross margin. Do not report gross merchandise value as revenue.

## 3. Competitive landscape: the capability stack is already occupied

YayaAiki is not entering an empty category. Its differentiation must be defined against the following alternatives.

| Competitor or substitute | What it already proves or offers | Strategic implication for YayaAiki |
|---|---|---|
| **Upwork** | Global profiles, proposals, work history, ratings, protected payments, dispute processes, local hiring pages, and enterprise services. Upwork reported FY2025 revenue of $787.8 million, GSV of $4.028 billion, and 785,000 active clients.[8] | A general verified-work marketplace is a feature-level comparison against a scaled incumbent. Win only where local execution, objective QA, or workflow integration is materially better. |
| **Workana** | Project posting, proposals, escrow, ratings, payments, managed talent, and transaction fees. Its published freelancer commission is tiered from 20% to 5%, illustrating the monetization trade-off between early transactions and repeat relationships.[9] | Trust and escrow are not novel. Local payment reliability and lower-friction Nigeria workflows must be specific and measurable. |
| **Andela** | Screened talent, technical assessments, matching, compliance, payments, long-term engagements, and managed services. Andela reports large enterprise and talent-community figures, which are company claims.[10] | The benchmark for vetted African talent plus service operations. A self-service directory will not be enough. |
| **Toptal** | Premium screening, a “top 3%” positioning, hand-selected talent, rapid matching, and trial-based guarantees.[11] | Quality assurance can command value, but YayaAiki must show a more cost-effective segment or more granular proof of completed work. |
| **Jobberman/TATC** | Nigerian recruitment, ATS workflows, relevance matching, assessments, paid listings, screening, executive search, and a pan-African network.[12] | Strong local employer and candidate distribution. Avoid becoming a generic job board; focus on execution rather than vacancies. |
| **TERAWORK and Gebeya** | Africa-focused talent, vetting or testing, matching, escrow or payment support, and regional workforce positioning.[13] | “African talent” and “verified freelancer” are already claimed. The evidence standard must be stronger than a profile badge. |
| **Deel and Workpay** | Contractor onboarding, compliance, payroll, employer-of-record or HR/payment capabilities across many countries.[14] | Cross-border payments and compliance are contested. Partner or integrate rather than assume YayaAiki should become a regulated pan-African wallet. |
| **BPOs, staffing firms, and internal teams** | Managed quality, accountability, procurement familiarity, and existing customer relationships. | The true alternative may be a managed vendor or an internal QA process, not another marketplace. Compare total delivered cost and accepted output. |

The competitive conclusion is not that YayaAiki cannot win. It is that “verified workers plus matching plus payment” is already table stakes. Upwork provides payment protection and work history; Andela provides screening and managed delivery; Jobberman provides local employer distribution; and payment/compliance vendors provide infrastructure. The entry wedge must be a **specific, outcome-linked workflow** that these alternatives serve poorly or expensively.

## 4. What could be defensible?

Branding, AI matching, an append-only database, WhatsApp access, or generic identity checks are not durable moats. They are useful features, but competitors can copy or buy them. Nigeria already has identity and verification vendors, including VerifyMe, Prembly, and Uiie, while Kenya has portable-history and employment-reference approaches such as Trustory and AjiraVerify.[15]

The strongest possible moat is a **permissioned evidence graph** that compounds with every completed job. Each record should connect, with consent, the work specification, worker identity, artifact provenance, timestamps, reviewer identity and calibration score, acceptance outcome, correction history, payment completion, dispute resolution, and later employer use. The value is not the record format. The value is the longitudinal predictive signal: which worker reliably completes which type of task, at what quality, speed, price, and dispute rate.

Four conditions are required for this to become defensible:

1. **Independent contribution.** Records must be corroborated by real buyers, reviewers, or institutions, not self-reported by workers alone.
2. **Outcome linkage.** Verification must predict accepted work, not merely verify identity or attendance.
3. **Governance and portability.** Workers need consent, access, correction, appeal, deletion where applicable, and a usable portable credential. Buyers need clear liability boundaries and auditability.
4. **Workflow embedding.** The evidence must sit inside customer procurement, staffing, QA, payout, and reporting processes so switching costs arise from operational value rather than lock-in alone.

This moat is operational and data-governance intensive. Nigeria’s Data Protection Commission recognizes rights including access, rectification, objection, portability, erasure, and protection from solely automated decisions.[16] YayaAiki must define controller and processor roles, lawful purpose, retention, security, human review, cross-border handling, and breach response before it publishes a reputation score. A payment partner does not remove YayaAiki’s own data and product obligations.

## 5. Sharp value proposition: Nigeria first, Africa next

### Nigeria-first proposition

> **For Nigerian businesses that repeatedly buy digital work, YayaAiki delivers accepted output—not just freelancer profiles—by turning each request into a measurable work order, routing it to screened professionals, independently checking evidence, and paying through a licensed local provider. Buyers get faster, auditable delivery and fewer failed batches; workers build portable proof of completed work without paying to be listed.**

This proposition is intentionally narrower than “Africa’s trust layer.” It promises a buyer outcome: accepted work with an audit trail and reliable payout. It should launch with one vertical where acceptance is objective, such as data labeling/AI evaluation, transcription/translation, or QA. The initial service level should specify turnaround, review method, rework policy, dispute process, payout timing, and buyer evidence export.

### Africa-next proposition

> **After proving the Nigeria workflow, YayaAiki can provide corridor-specific verified-work and payout infrastructure for African buyers and workers, using local-currency settlement, local compliance partners, portable consented work records, and country-specific evidence standards.**

“Africa next” must mean country-by-country expansion, not a single continental wallet. PAPSS can support connected regulated participants and local-currency payments, but it is a rail, not proof of YayaAiki’s licensing, access, KYC/AML capability, pricing, or coverage.[17] Expansion should require a local payment/compliance partner, privacy and labor analysis, supported identity methods, currency policy, dispute operations, and evidence that the Nigeria quality model transfers.

## 6. A 90-day traction plan before fundraising

The objective is to create evidence of **paid repeat demand and positive contribution margin**, not registrations, social engagement, or a large worker waitlist.

### Days 1–15: narrow the claim and pre-sell the workflow

Choose one task family, one buyer persona, and one Nigeria corridor. A suitable starting segment is a data/AI vendor, research firm, BPO, agency, NGO, or enterprise operations team that already buys repeatable batches. Interview at least 20 buyers and 30 qualified workers, but treat interviews as discovery rather than traction.

Define a one-page work-order contract: scope, acceptance criteria, evidence required, review method, rework limits, price, payout timing, data handling, and dispute process. Secure written pilot commitments from at least three buyers, ideally with deposits or purchase orders. Select one primary and one backup licensed payment provider; obtain counsel’s written mapping of custody, payout, KYC/AML, tax, data, and contractor obligations.

Build only the minimum operating system: buyer intake, worker screening, task assignment, evidence upload, reviewer queue, payout state, dispute log, and exportable audit record. Keep funds out of YayaAiki custody initially. A web evidence/reviewer console is required even if WhatsApp handles intake.

### Days 16–45: run paid production, manually but measurably

Complete the first paid batches with a deliberately small worker pool. Use two reviewers on a sample of tasks to estimate inter-reviewer agreement. Record every exception: unclear specification, late delivery, rework, reviewer disagreement, fraud signal, payment failure, support contact, and dispute outcome.

Target at least 100–300 paid work orders or an equivalent volume of buyer-defined batches, depending on task granularity. Do not fabricate a universal target; the relevant denominator is enough volume to estimate quality and margin for the selected workflow. Invoice for the workflow even if early delivery is heavily assisted. Founder-led operations are acceptable during discovery, but every manual step must be time-measured.

### Days 46–75: test repeatability and pricing

Ask each pilot buyer to place a second order without founder persuasion beyond the normal account process. Test a price increase or a buyer fee for verification/auditability. Compare the buyer’s prior alternative—internal team, BPO, Upwork, direct contractor, or agency—on total cost, turnaround, rework, and accepted-output rate.

Calibrate reviewers and automate only checks that have sufficient labeled examples. Test worker payout SLA and failure rate across the selected payment route. Run adverse-impact and correction tests for any ranking or reputation signal. Ensure workers can see the basis for an adverse decision and submit a correction or appeal.

### Days 76–90: decision gate and fundraising packet

Prepare a cohort-level data room containing customer contracts and invoices, repeat-order evidence, work-order metrics, reviewer calibration, payment reports, dispute log, privacy/security controls, partner agreements, and a fully loaded unit-economics model. Fundraising should follow only if the pilot shows recurring paid demand and a path to positive contribution margin.

Recommended go/no-go thresholds are directional rather than universal: **3–5 paying anchor customers; repeat purchase from a majority of pilots; independently sampled verification precision and recall at a pre-agreed level; high on-time acceptance; low dispute and payment-failure rates; positive contribution margin after review, support, fraud, and payment costs; and at least some records later accepted by an unrelated employer or buyer.** The exact thresholds must be set by task risk and buyer economics before the test begins.

## 7. Claims that must not be made without evidence

YayaAiki should use a claim register. The following statements should be prohibited in marketing, investor materials, and partner sales until supported by auditable evidence:

- “We have product-market fit,” “we are infrastructure,” or “we are Africa’s trusted work layer.”
- Any number of customers, workers, completed jobs, GMV, worker earnings, retention, repeat rate, fill rate, or payment volume not backed by dated records and a defined denominator.
- “Verified professional” without specifying whether verification means identity, skills, work sample, client-confirmed outcome, employment history, or payment completion.
- “Independent verification” unless reviewer independence, calibration, sampling, conflict policy, and accuracy are documented.
- “Guaranteed quality,” “fraud-free,” “compliant,” or “secure” without scope, controls, audit evidence, and a defined remedy.
- “Fast payouts,” “instant payments,” or “pan-African payments” without country-specific payout SLA, failure rate, licensed provider, supported currencies, and corridor coverage.
- “Portable reputation” unless a worker can access, correct, appeal, and use the record outside the originating workflow, with consent and governance documented.
- “Lower cost than Upwork, Andela, Jobberman, a BPO, or direct hiring” without a like-for-like comparison that includes review, support, payment, fraud, compliance, and rework costs.
- Any claim that PAPSS, a payment partner, or a KYC vendor automatically gives YayaAiki continental coverage or regulatory approval.[17]
- Any claim that the platform verifies employment, qualification, character, or safety when it has only verified completion of a particular work order.
- Any claim of universal inclusion through WhatsApp, web, voice, or USSD while voice and USSD remain future features and connectivity/device constraints remain material.[4] [5]

The current public YayaAiki site does not disclose customer count, completed work, GMV, take rate, repeat rate, review accuracy, payout SLA, named payment partner, licence, or customer references.[4] The illustrated work-order identifiers and append-only log are product examples, not traction evidence.

## 8. Product and engineering priorities

### P0: make one work order trustworthy end to end

Build a strict work-order schema with versioned scope, acceptance criteria, deadline, price, evidence requirements, reviewer assignment, payout state, and dispute state. Make every state transition auditable. Separate **identity evidence, skill evidence, work-output evidence, reviewer evidence, and payment evidence** so the product never collapses distinct claims into a generic “verified” badge.

Build buyer intake, worker assignment, evidence capture, reviewer queue, rework loop, dispute handling, payout reconciliation, and audit export before broad profiles or social features. Support WhatsApp-assisted intake, but keep evidence and review in a controlled web console. Use idempotent payment webhooks, immutable event IDs, reconciliation jobs, retry queues, and explicit failure states. YayaAiki should never imply that a payout succeeded merely because a request was sent.

### P0: governance, privacy, and abuse resistance

Implement consent and purpose notices; data minimization; retention and deletion policies; role-based access; encryption; tenant isolation; export, correction, and appeal flows; reviewer conflict declarations; duplicate-identity checks; evidence hashing; anomaly detection; and breach-response procedures. Maintain a human decision path for ranking, rejection, and adverse action. Contractually define data controller/processor roles with customers and vendors.

Do not build a public reputation score until there is enough evidence to test bias, retaliation, and error correction. Start with buyer-scoped records and worker-controlled sharing. This reduces the harm of a mistaken or disputed record while the evidence standard matures.

### P1: reviewer quality and unit economics

Create reviewer calibration tasks, double-review samples, reviewer-level precision/recall, disagreement analysis, reviewer payment controls, and an escalation queue. Instrument reviewer minutes and support minutes per work order. The core engineering question is not whether AI can classify evidence; it is whether automation reduces total cost without reducing acceptance reliability.

Use AI only for bounded assistive functions such as duplicate detection, evidence completeness checks, routing, transcription, or anomaly triage. Keep human review for consequential acceptance and adverse decisions until measured error is acceptable. Store model version, prompt/configuration, evidence used, and human override for auditability.

### P1: payments and partner abstraction

Integrate licensed providers behind a payment-state abstraction that supports authorization, hold where lawful, release, refund, failure, reversal, reconciliation, and beneficiary notification. Keep money movement with the regulated provider initially. Store only necessary payment identifiers and separate payment data from public work history. Add local-currency pricing and clear FX treatment before cross-border expansion.

### P2: portability and integrations after proof

Once the closed-loop workflow has repeat buyers, add a worker-controlled credential export with evidence provenance, consent receipts, correction history, and expiry. Offer buyer APIs or webhooks for work orders, acceptance, payout status, and audit exports. Integrate with ATS, procurement, HR, or BPO systems only when a real customer requests the integration and the switching value is demonstrated.

## Final recommendation

**Proceed with a narrow Nigeria pilot, not a broad launch.** The opportunity is real because informal work, outsourced digital demand, quality uncertainty, and payment friction coexist. Profitability is plausible only when YayaAiki sells accepted, repeatable outcomes at a price that covers worker pay and the full cost of verification, fraud, support, payment, and compliance. The company should compete against Upwork, Workana, Andela, Jobberman, regional marketplaces, payment/compliance vendors, and BPOs on one measurable workflow—not on an abstract Africa-wide trust narrative.

The next financing decision should be evidence-gated. If the 90-day pilot produces repeat paid buyers, independently measured quality, reliable payouts, low dispute loss, and positive contribution margin without founder-led exceptions, raise to deepen the Nigeria wedge. If it produces worker signups but not repeat paid work, or revenue but negative contribution margin after review and support, narrow the task or stop. Until then, the correct description is **“promising hypothesis with a credible Nigeria wedge,” not “validated infrastructure business.”**
